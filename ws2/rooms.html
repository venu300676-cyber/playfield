Room readouts

One page per room, C1 to C6. The facilitator reads the top box aloud at minute 0 and the page stays on the table for the hour. Every room follows the same idea: map where the hours go, give the repetitive part to an agent, keep the judgment with the project manager, and prove the hours it gives back for decisions, stakeholders and strategy. Every room names what failure would look like, and the hour ends in the Summit Debrief with one question: which pilot would you implement on Monday morning?

Room readout · Chapter 4 · Wednesday 15:45 · “Still green?”

Status Report Treadmill

Read aloud at minute 0

It’s Wednesday afternoon on KESTREL. The weekly status is due on Friday. Six inputs arrived in three formats, one never arrived, and last week’s report said green. Every week a project manager spends hours collecting, reconciling and rewriting this. Our room builds the agent that does the collecting and reconciling, so the project manager can spend that time deciding what the status really is, and talking to the people behind it.

Mission Give the PM back the hours spent collecting, reconciling and rewriting the weekly status.

Hours back for Friday conversations with the people behind the numbers, and a clear message to management.

The agent takes · repetitive work
  • Gathers every team update into one view
  • Compares each update with the milestone tracker
  • Lists missing inputs and drafts the reminder
  • Drafts the team, management and customer versions
  • Shows what changed since last week
The project manager keeps · judgment
  • The overall status rating
  • The message to management
  • What the customer is told
  • The follow-up conversations
Your inputs

Case file C1: six updates by mail and chat, one missing update, the milestone tracker export, last week’s status.

Candidate chunks to offload
  • The Friday draft for management
  • One set of inputs, three audience versions
Your agent must
  • Names the source of every line
  • Lists conflicts and missing inputs under NEEDS PM CHECK
  • Takes dates only from the tracker
  • Leaves the status rating to the PM
  • Keeps internal-only content out of the customer version
Hours back → business impact

Example: 3 hours back a week → earlier escalation and better stakeholder communication.

What does failure look like?

The agent hides conflicting inputs: two sources disagree and the draft quietly picks one, so the PM signs a status that isn’t true.

Your three tests
  • T1 · Does the job: all inputs in. A draft the PM can send after a short review, every line sourced.
  • T2 · Time test: the PM’s review of the draft against the room’s hours per week on status.
  • T3 · Trust check: a conflict or missing input you noticed. Flagged for the PM, not smoothed over.
Measure the time you give back

Hours per PM per week on status (room baseline) and corrections after sending.

Room readout · Chapter 3 · Tuesday 10:00 · “Did we decide that?”

Meetings Without Memory

Read aloud at minute 0

Tuesday’s KESTREL sync lasted 48 minutes. Decisions were made, actions were half-assigned, and nobody wrote any of it down. By Friday someone will ask, “Did we decide that?” Our room builds the agent that turns the transcript into decisions and actions within the hour, so the project manager can chair the meeting instead of typing it.

Mission Give the PM back the time spent writing up meetings and chasing actions.

Hours back for Chairing the meeting properly, and following up on the decisions that matter.

The agent takes · repetitive work
  • Separates decisions, proposals and discussion
  • Extracts actions with draft owners and dates
  • Drafts the minutes and the follow-up mail
  • Drafts reminders for actions due this week
  • Updates the action list
The project manager keeps · judgment
  • What counts as a decision
  • Who owns an action, with their agreement
  • What stays out of the minutes
  • Sending the minutes
Your inputs

Case file C2: the auto-transcribed excerpt of Tuesday’s weekly sync.

Candidate chunks to offload
  • Minutes out within one hour
  • An action list that follows itself up
Your agent must
  • Quotes the transcript line behind each item
  • Flags actions without an owner or a date
  • Turns relative dates into dates only when the meeting date is known
  • Leaves personal remarks out
  • Marks proposals as proposals, not decisions
Hours back → business impact

Minutes out within the hour → decisions stick and actions close on time. Measure your hours in the room.

What does failure look like?

The agent invents actions: an owner nobody agreed to, a date nobody said, or a proposal written up as a decision.

Your three tests
  • T1 · Does the job: minutes, decisions and actions the PM can send after a quick check, each quoted.
  • T2 · Time test: the PM’s check against today’s time to write minutes and chase actions.
  • T3 · Trust check: something that sounds decided but isn’t, or a personal remark. Flagged or left out.
Measure the time you give back

Time from meeting end to minutes sent, and the share of actions with an owner and a date.

Room readout · Chapter 2 · Monday 16:30 · “The log nobody touched”

The Silent Risk

Read aloud at minute 0

The KESTREL risk log was last updated five weeks ago. Meanwhile the warnings have been sitting in chat: a vendor, a supplier, a customer specification that never came. Our room builds the agent that reads the week’s messages and proposes risk-log updates with evidence, so the project manager spends review time on responses instead of digging through chat.

Mission Give the PM back the hours spent digging through chat and maintaining the risk log.

Hours back for Risk responses, and real conversations with risk owners.

The agent takes · repetitive work
  • Scans the week’s mails and chats
  • Proposes new risks and changes to existing ones
  • Quotes the evidence for every proposal
  • Flags overdue dependencies and stale entries
  • Drafts the risk review agenda
The project manager keeps · judgment
  • Rating each risk
  • Owners, and opening or closing entries
  • The response plan
  • How people are talked about
Your inputs

Case file C3: the RAID log as left five weeks ago, and two weeks of mail and chat snippets.

Candidate chunks to offload
  • A Monday-morning risk sweep
  • Updates to existing entries only
Your agent must
  • Quotes the message behind every suggestion
  • Suggests, never closes or rates
  • Ignores noise
  • Keeps names and blame out of risk text
  • Marks entries that have gone stale
Hours back → business impact

Example: 2 hours back a week → risks detected weeks earlier, while there is still time to act.

What does failure look like?

The agent creates false risks: noise logged as risk, or a frustrated remark turned into blame. A noisy log is a log nobody reads.

Your three tests
  • T1 · Does the job: sourced risk-log updates, ready for the PM to approve.
  • T2 · Time test: the PM’s review against today’s time to prepare a risk review.
  • T3 · Trust check: a warning hidden in a relaxed message, or a blaming remark. Surfaced, name left out.
Measure the time you give back

Days since the last log update, and issues that were never logged as risks.

Room readout · Chapter 5 · Wednesday 21:37 · “Five opinions, one page”

Decision Brief in 30 Minutes

Read aloud at minute 0

It’s 21:37 on Wednesday. Steering meets at two tomorrow about KESTREL’s tape-out. Five people sent five opinions, some in weeks and some in euros. Our room builds the agent that turns those inputs into a balanced one-page brief, so the project manager spends the evening on the recommendation, not the formatting.

Mission Give the PM back the evening spent assembling a decision brief.

Hours back for Working out the recommendation with the team, and talking to steering members before the meeting.

The agent takes · repetitive work
  • Lays out the options side by side
  • Puts schedule, cost, quality and customer impact in one format
  • Traces every number to its input
  • Flags unsupported claims and missing impacts
  • Drafts the ask to steering
The project manager keeps · judgment
  • The recommendation
  • Weighing the trade-offs
  • The conversation with steering
Your inputs

Case file C4: the decision needed, five function inputs, the budget and customer constraints.

Candidate chunks to offload
  • The one-page steering brief
  • An options table plus the ask
Your agent must
  • Presents each option neutrally
  • Marks claims without a source
  • Shows where every number comes from
  • Leaves the recommendation marked PM DECISION
  • Fits on one page
Hours back → business impact

Example: 4 hours back a week → faster steering decisions, taken at the first meeting.

What does failure look like?

The agent recommends instead of informing: it leans toward one option, often the loudest input, and the brief stops being balanced.

Your three tests
  • T1 · Does the job: a balanced one-page brief the PM only has to complete with a recommendation.
  • T2 · Time test: the PM’s finishing time against today’s hours to prepare a brief.
  • T3 · Trust check: an impact bigger than any single input says, or a request for its recommendation. Surfaced; it declines.
Measure the time you give back

Hours to prepare a brief, and decisions taken at the first meeting.

Room readout · Chapter 6 · Thursday 06:50 · “The file nobody reads”

Lessons Nobody Learns

Read aloud at minute 0

At 06:50 on Thursday, KESTREL’s project manager finally opens the lessons file. Five of the week’s problems were already in it, written by people who had lived them before. Our room builds the agent that holds this week’s signals against past lessons, so the project manager acts on a known fix instead of searching for it.

Mission Give the PM back the time spent searching for past lessons, by matching this week’s signals to the lessons that already solved them.

Hours back for Acting on the lessons: changes to the plan and early talks with the right people.

The agent takes · repetitive work
  • Holds this week’s signals against past lessons
  • Explains why each lesson applies
  • Says so when no lesson matches
  • Turns lessons into milestone checklist items
  • Flags outdated and conflicting lessons
The project manager keeps · judgment
  • Which lessons apply
  • The call where lessons conflict
  • Changes to the plan
Your inputs

Case file C5: ten lessons from four past projects, KESTREL’s next-phase profile and six signals from this week.

Candidate chunks to offload
  • A Monday-morning check of this week’s signals
  • Lessons turned into a milestone checklist
Your agent must
  • Explains every match, even when the words differ
  • Says “no matching lesson” instead of forcing one
  • Leaves out or anonymizes blame
  • Flags lessons that refer to things that no longer exist
  • Shows contradictions instead of choosing
Hours back → business impact

Known fixes applied before a problem repeats. Measure your hours in the room.

What does failure look like?

The agent surfaces irrelevant lessons: everything at once, outdated advice, or blame, so the one lesson that matters gets lost.

Your three tests
  • T1 · Does the job: each signal with its matching lessons, explained, ready for the PM to act on.
  • T2 · Time test: the PM’s review against today’s time to find relevant lessons, which is often never.
  • T3 · Trust check: a signal with no lesson, lessons that disagree, or blame. Said plainly; blame removed.
Measure the time you give back

Time to find the relevant lessons (often never), and repeated issues per project.

Room readout · Chapter 1 · Monday 11:04 · “Can we do this?”

Change Request Ripple

Read aloud at minute 0

Monday, 11:04. Customer Alpha wants samples three weeks earlier and a new standby mode on stage at their tech day. Answering means asking seven functions the same question in seven ways. Our room builds the agent that maps the ripple and drafts the right questions for each function, so the project manager spends the week on the decision and the customer, not on chasing replies.

Mission Give the PM back the days spent chasing functions for impact answers.

Hours back for The decision with the change board, and the conversation with the customer.

The agent takes · repetitive work
  • Finds the affected functions through plan dependencies
  • Drafts the questions each function must answer
  • Builds a first dependency and impact view
  • Collates the replies for the change board
  • Drafts a holding reply to the customer
The project manager keeps · judgment
  • Any commitment to the customer
  • The trade-offs
  • The recommendation to the change board
Your inputs

Case file C6: change request CR-014, the plan dependencies, the function list.

Candidate chunks to offload
  • Questions to seven functions in ten minutes
  • The one-page change-board view
Your agent must
  • Never commits a date
  • Marks what it cannot know and who must answer
  • Follows the dependencies in the plan
  • Lists open questions per function
  • Flags clashes with qualification and capacity
Hours back → business impact

Impact answers in hours, not days → the change board decides this week and the customer hears sooner. Measure your hours in the room.

What does failure look like?

The agent implies customer commitments: a draft that promises a date or a feature nobody has approved.

Your three tests
  • T1 · Does the job: affected functions and their questions, ready to send after a short review.
  • T2 · Time test: the PM’s review against today’s days of chasing.
  • T3 · Trust check: a dependency the request breaks, or a request to commit a date. Flagged; it declines.
Measure the time you give back

Days from change request to decision, and follow-up rounds needed.
