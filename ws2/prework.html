Prologue · Monday · CW38

The handover

Tape-out in 14 days

The coffee machine on floor 3 is broken again, so you start your first day as KESTREL’s project manager with a paper cup of something brown from the ground floor and a calendar that someone else has already filled.

Jonas left on Friday. He didn’t leave the company; he moved to a program two buildings over that needed him more urgently than KESTREL did, which tells you something about KESTREL, or about the other program. You aren’t sure which yet. His handover was a 25-minute Teams call, most of it spent on a camera that wouldn’t start, and a folder called KESTREL_handover_FINAL_v3. On the monitor he no longer uses there is a yellow note:

“Everything’s in the folder. The lessons file is in there too. Nobody reads it. Good luck with Customer Alpha. J.”

Here is what you know. KESTREL is a new sensor-hub chip and the evaluation kit that goes with it. Its lead customer, Customer Alpha, has a design-in window that closes at the end of the year. Win it, and KESTREL becomes the reference part for a whole product family. Tape-out is in calendar week 40, fourteen days from now.

11:04
Chapter 1 · Monday

Can we do this?

Room card C6 · Change Request Ripple

Sofia’s message arrives with a subject line in capitals.

From: Sofia (Product Marketing)
Subject: FW: CR-014 · URGENT · Can we do this??

Customer Alpha has a tech day in week 48. They want samples in week 47 instead of week 50, and they want the evaluation kit to show an “ultra-low-power standby” mode on stage. “They need an answer this week,” Sofia writes. “Big opportunity. Can you confirm?”

You open the plan. Tape-out, then five weeks in the fab, then first samples in week 46. Testing them needs a load board that, you now discover, the vendor has just moved from week 41 to week 43. Qualification starts in week 48. The software team that owns the evaluation kit is at half capacity until week 44. And somewhere in a design document you haven’t read yet is a line saying the chip’s ROM content is frozen at tape-out.

Can we do this? You genuinely don’t know. The honest answer lives in seven heads: Design, Verification, Test, Software, Quality, Operations and Marketing. You start typing the first of seven emails, each asking roughly the same question in a slightly different way, and you already know how this goes. The answers will come on Thursday, in seven formats. Two of them will say “depends”. One will never arrive.

Customer Alpha is waiting. So is your 11:30.

Chapter 2 · Monday

The log nobody touched

Room card C3 · The Silent Risk

Between meetings you finally open the RAID log. Last updated: CW33. Five weeks ago. Three risks, one issue, one dependency, and a status column that reads more like a memory than a measurement.

R2, load board vendor capacity, was closed in week 30. You scroll back through your chats. Priya, last week: the vendor’s test line is “overbooked again”. D1, Customer Alpha’s use-case spec, was due in week 36. Sofia, two weeks ago: “soon, they’re reorganizing.” Chen, this morning, in passing: the supplier has moved your substrate order to their next allocation cycle, but it “should still be fine”. Daniel, with good news for once: the qualification lab has confirmed your week-48 slot. Lukas has reported the broken coffee machine as a productivity risk.

At 22:10 your phone lights up. Aiko’s design team is working its third weekend in a row. There is a sentence about a colleague and some constraints back in July that you read twice and decide not to forward to anyone.

Nobody hid anything from you today. Everything was said, somewhere. It just never became a risk that anyone owns. You wonder how many of the surprises waiting in week 46 are already sitting in your chat history.

Chapter 3 · Tuesday

Did we decide that?

Room card C2 · Meetings Without Memory

The weekly sync. Seven people, one of them on a train. You chair and take notes at the same time, which means you do both badly.

Aiko: three timing paths are still failing on the power-management block. She thinks they’ll close by Wednesday; if not, “we need to talk about options.” Sofia wants to propose to steering that you keep week 40 “no matter what”. Daniel isn’t comfortable with that without a quality view. You hear yourself say “let’s not decide that here,” and ask Aiko for a status on Wednesday evening so you can prepare options for Thursday’s steering meeting.

Priya: the load board is late, week 43 now. “Someone needs to check with the vendor.” Everyone agrees it’s important. Nobody says who. Lukas checks that the SDK only goes to Customer Alpha after the quality review, and Daniel confirms and promises the review “by end of next week”. Chen mentions, quietly, that he is out for two weeks from the 3rd for a family reason, and that his cover isn’t confirmed. And almost as an afterthought you decide something real: from now on, every evaluation kit shipment gets an export-control check before it leaves the building.

Forty-eight minutes. At 10:49 you are already in the next call. The transcript sits in the meeting chat, two thousand words long. By Friday somebody will ask, “Did we decide that?” and you will not be sure.

Chapter 4 · Wednesday

Still green?

Room card C1 · Status Report Treadmill

Management wants the weekly status by Friday noon. Last week’s report said GREEN, in Jonas’s confident formatting.

The inputs arrive the way they always do. Aiko’s came by email on Monday, written before anyone knew how Wednesday would go. Marco, in chat: 92% coverage against a target of 95. Priya, by email: test program development “on track”, and in the next sentence, the load board now expected in week 43. Lukas, in chat: SDK beta done, party emoji, and “FYI” a clause from Customer Alpha’s contract about a demo and a penalty that legal is still reviewing. Daniel needs samples by week 46 to start qualification in 48. Chen: “No issues.” Sofia: nothing. You send a reminder. Then another.

The milestone tracker still shows First samples + test ready in week 46, unchanged. You look at it for a long time.

Is KESTREL still green? You could copy last week’s report, change the date and move on. People do. Or you could reconcile six inputs in three formats, one missing voice and a tracker that may no longer tell the truth, and then write it all three times: once for the team, once for management and once, very carefully, for the customer. That is your Wednesday evening. It was Jonas’s too, every week, for a year.

Chapter 5 · Wednesday

Five opinions, one page

Room card C4 · Decision Brief in 30 Minutes
From: Aiko (Design lead) · 21:37
Subject: PMU · not closed

A clean fix needs three more weeks. Or you tape out on time and repair it later with a metal-layer respin, about six weeks after first samples. Aiko recommends the delay.

By 22:30 you have five opinions and a steering meeting at 14:00 tomorrow. Sofia: the customer’s design-in window closes at year end; “if we are late, they will walk away.” Daniel: qualifying silicon with a known issue means qualifying it all over again after any fix. Chen: a respin mask set costs about €180k; a three-week slip costs nothing directly, but it loses the wafer start slot, and the next slot is two weeks later. Lukas: a firmware workaround might hide the problem in low-power mode. Two weeks of work, not validated. Your remaining budget reserve is €150k.

Steering doesn’t want five opinions. They want the options, the impacts, a clear ask and your recommendation, on one page. Everyone has told you what they think. Nobody has told you what it adds up to. That part is your job.

It is 22:41.

Chapter 6 · Thursday

The file nobody reads

Room card C5 · Lessons Nobody Learns

You wake before the alarm. Because you can’t face the brief yet, you open Jonas’s folder and, for the first time, the lessons file.

Four past projects: HERON, OSPREY, PLOVER, WREN. Ten lessons, written by tired people at the end of something.

ATE hardware ordered after tape-out arrived five weeks late. You think of your load board. Customer samples shipped before qualification caused two escalations. You think of CR-014. SDK released without quality review led to customer-found bugs. You think of Tuesday. Export-control classification done late blocked shipments for three weeks. You think of the decision you made almost by accident.

There is a lesson about a tool the company retired last year, and another about a test engineer called John that is less a lesson than a grudge. Two lessons disagree completely about shipping early samples.

Somebody learned all of this the hard way, more than once. What if this file had found you on Monday morning, at the moment you needed it, instead of sitting in a folder?

None of this is the job you were hired for. That job is getting a team of smart, tired people to a good decision, on time. This is the work that stands between you and it.

On 5 November your breakout room takes one moment from this week.
