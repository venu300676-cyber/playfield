Pain to Pilot

Breakout kit: one PM pain point, one AI assistant, one tested Pilot Pack per room

Every room takes one job that eats a project manager’s week, maps where the hours go, and builds an agent that takes the repetitive part. After 60 minutes it leaves with a Pilot Pack: the agent’s instructions, the decisions the PM keeps, the hours it gives back and where that time goes, one guardrail, an owner and a first test.

Topic

Offload the repetitive part of one PM job to an agent, so the project manager gets time back for decisions, stakeholders and strategy.

Goal

Each mixed-experience room maps where the hours go, builds an agent for the biggest repetitive chunk, and proves the time it returns, while the PM keeps every judgment.

Outcome

One Pilot Pack per room with a measured “hours back per PM per week”, in the same format everywhere, so packs can be compared and the biggest time returns piloted first.

What every room delivers

One Pilot Pack, three levels of ambition

Every room must reach Level 1. That alone meets the WS2 definition of done Confirmed: one AI use case, an owner and a first test per room. Levels 2 and 3 depend on tool access and on how experienced the room is, so a room of AI beginners and a room of power users both finish with something real.

Level 1

Designed

Required for every room

Pilot Pack complete. Agent instructions written. Owner, first test, metric and guardrail named.

Works with pen and paper.

Level 2

Tested

Target for most rooms

Instructions run against the sample data, either in the approved AI tool or in the “Be the agent” paper test. Three tests recorded and at least one fix made.

Works without a tool.

Level 3

Working agent

Depends on tool decision

Saved as a reusable agent or prompt in the approved tool, so the owner can run it the next working day.

Needs agent-building access in the room.

What “agent” means in this kit

An agent here is a reusable set of instructions with fixed inputs, rules, an output format and a human checkpoint, which any PM can run again. It can live as a saved prompt, a custom assistant, or an agent in whatever tool is approved. The instructions are the asset. The tool can be swapped.

The pilot-ready test

From the knowledge base: a case is pilot-ready only if all six are present. The Pilot Pack form checks them live.

C1

Status Report Treadmill

How might we cut the time a PM spends turning scattered updates into a trustworthy weekly status, without handing the status call to the AI?

Theme: Metrics and value measurementRoom fit: entry and mixedBuild effort: medium
Who feels it
The PM who compiles the report; team leads who get chased for input; managers and customers who read reports that vary in format and quality.
What happens today
Updates arrive in different formats (mail, chat, tracker) and at different times. The PM chases missing input, reconciles it with the plan, then rewrites the same content for the team, for management and sometimes for the customer.
Baseline to measure in the room
  • Hours per week per PM spent on status
  • Number of audience versions
  • Chasing messages per week
  • How often a sent status is corrected later
Trusted sources
Plan or schedule tool (the source of truth for dates), issue tracker, RAID log, team updates.
Constraints
The overall status rating and the message to management stay with the PM. Dates come from the plan, never from generated text. Customer versions carry no internal-only content.
Questions to explore
  • Which step is the real time sink: collecting, reconciling or writing?
  • How would the assistant notice an update that contradicts the plan?
  • What does the PM check before pressing Send?
  • What should happen when an update is missing?
Stretch goal
One set of inputs, three audience versions, with a list of what differs between them and why.

C2

Meetings Without Memory

How might we make sure every decision and action from a project meeting is captured, owned and followed up within an hour of the meeting ending?

Theme: Practical AI adoption and approved toolsRoom fit: entryBuild effort: low
Who feels it
The PM who chairs and takes notes at the same time; attendees; people who missed the meeting.
What happens today
Notes are written while chairing. Minutes go out late or not at all. Actions have no owner or date. Decisions are re-opened the next week because nobody recorded them.
Baseline to measure in the room
  • Time from meeting end to minutes sent
  • Share of actions with an owner and a due date
  • Decisions re-discussed per month
Trusted sources
Meeting transcript or notes (only where recording and transcription are allowed), action tracker, decision log.
Constraints
Transcription needs consent and must follow local policy. The PM confirms what counts as a decision. Nobody gets an action they did not accept. Personal remarks stay out of the minutes.
Questions to explore
  • How do you tell a decision from a discussion?
  • Who confirms owners and dates?
  • Where do actions live after the meeting?
  • What do people who were absent need?
Stretch goal
Draft follow-up reminders for every action due in the coming week.

Facilitator only · what the agent must flag in the C2 data
  • Sofia’s “let’s propose to steering that we keep CW47” is a proposal the PM explicitly deferred. It is not a decision.
  • “Someone needs to check with the vendor” has no owner. It must be flagged, not assigned to Priya by guesswork.
  • “End of next week” is relative. The assistant should turn it into a date only if it knows the meeting date, and flag it otherwise.
  • Chen’s family situation is personal. Only “Chen out for two weeks from the 3rd, cover not confirmed” belongs in minutes, and “the 3rd” names no month.
  • The real decisions: an export-control check before every eval kit shipment, and the SDK release to Customer Alpha gated on the quality review (re-confirmed).
C3

The Silent Risk

How might we spot emerging risks in everyday project conversation before they become issues, while people keep ownership of every risk?

Theme: Risk and quality in AI-augmented projectsRoom fit: mixedBuild effort: medium
Who feels it
The PM, risk owners, and managers who review risk.
What happens today
The RAID log is updated just before reviews. Risks are mentioned in mails and stand-ups but never logged. Ratings go stale. Milestone reviews bring surprises.
Baseline to measure in the room
  • Days since the last RAID update
  • Issues last quarter that were never logged as risks
  • Time spent preparing a risk review
Trusted sources
RAID log, team updates, mail and chat (within policy), plan.
Constraints
The assistant suggests; people rate, own and close risks. Every suggestion quotes its source. No blame on individuals. Supplier information may be confidential.
Questions to explore
  • What makes a message a signal rather than noise?
  • What would make a PM trust a suggested risk?
  • How often should it run?
  • Who approves a new entry in the log?
Stretch goal
Suggest updates to existing entries as well as new ones, with the evidence for each change.

C4

Decision Brief in 30 Minutes

How might we help a PM turn conflicting inputs into a clear, balanced decision brief for management, fast, without the AI making the recommendation?

Theme: Decision-making with AI and human accountabilityRoom fit: mixedBuild effort: medium
Who feels it
The PM; the decision makers in steering or management; the functions whose input shapes the options.
What happens today
An escalation needs options, impacts and a clear ask. The PM spends hours collecting views and writing. Briefs come out long, tilted toward one function, or without the actual ask, and the decision slips to the next meeting.
Baseline to measure in the room
  • Hours to prepare one decision brief
  • Meetings before a decision is taken
  • How often management asks for more information
Trusted sources
Function inputs, plan, cost data, decision log.
Constraints
The recommendation is the PM’s. Every number traces to an input. Options are presented fairly. Commercial and cost data may be confidential.
Questions to explore
  • What does a decision maker need on one page?
  • How do you stop the assistant from favoring the loudest input?
  • Which numbers must come from systems?
Link to the summit
Complements the Driving Decision Making session. If that session shares a decision framework, rooms reuse it here. Assumption
Stretch goal
A pre-mortem line per option: “If we choose this and it fails, the most likely reason is …”

C5

Lessons Nobody Learns

How might we bring the right past lessons to a PM at the moment they can still act on them, instead of filing them at project close?

Theme: Redefining the PM roleRoom fit: mixedBuild effort: medium
Who feels it
PMs entering a new phase; the PMO; functional leads who keep solving the same problems.
What happens today
Lessons are collected at closure in slides or spreadsheets and rarely read again. The same problems repeat, and finding the relevant lesson is slow.
Baseline to measure in the room
  • Did you look at past lessons at your last phase start?
  • Repeated issues you can name
  • Time to find a relevant lesson
Trusted sources
Lessons-learned repository, retrospectives, closure reports, ticket history.
Constraints
Lessons may name people or suppliers and must be anonymized. Old lessons may be outdated. The PM decides what applies.
Questions to explore
  • What makes a lesson reusable: context, trigger, action?
  • At which project moment should it appear?
  • How would you know a lesson was actually applied?
Stretch goal
Turn the top three lessons into checklist items for the next milestone review.

C6

Change Request Ripple

How might we see the ripple effect of a change request across functions in hours instead of days, so the change board decides on facts?

Theme: Stakeholder and team dynamicsRoom fit: experiencedBuild effort: high
Who feels it
The PM, functional leads, the change control board, customer-facing teams.
What happens today
A change request arrives. The PM mails each function. Answers come late and in different shapes. Schedule, cost and risk impact are assembled by hand, and the decision slips while the customer waits.
Baseline to measure in the room
  • Days from change request to decision
  • Functions contacted
  • Follow-up rounds needed
Trusted sources
Plan with dependencies, specification, resource plan, change request form.
Constraints
Each function owns its impact estimate. The assistant drafts questions and a first dependency view; it never commits dates. Customer commitments need approval.
Questions to explore
  • Which functions does the change touch, and how would you know?
  • What must each function answer?
  • What does the change board need on one page?
Stretch goal
A draft reply to the customer that commits to nothing except a decision date.

Regional swap card

A region may replace one challenge with a local pain point it submitted, if the new card meets the same standard: a named pain, users, a baseline to measure, trusted sources, constraints, open questions, and fictional or approved sample data with at least two traps. Swap cards need WS2 review before the kit freeze, so rooms never start from a blank page.

The 60-minute screenplay

This flow keeps the stages of the 10 September proposal (orient, validate, shape, pressure-test, recommend). Because the topic is given, the 7-minute selection step becomes build time. If WS2 prefers rooms to choose between two or three cards, take the 5 minutes from Design.

Minute 0 of 60

Roles

Facilitator

Runs the process, protects time and voices. Needs no AI expertise and never writes the answer.

Scribe and team lead

Owns the Pilot Pack, synthesizes, gives the report-out.

Timekeeper

Runs the timer, calls each checkpoint, defends the last 10 minutes.

Builder (1–2)

Hands on the approved AI tool. Narrates what they type. Named before the summit so access is checked in advance.

Challenger (1–2)

The room’s AI steward. Tests value, data, confidentiality, quality, tool approval and accountability.

Everyone

Brings a real example, challenges assumptions, plays the agent in the paper test.

Rooms of five or fewer combine Facilitator with Timekeeper, and Scribe with Challenger.

Minute by minute

  1. 00:00–05:00Orient
    Say
    “In the next 60 minutes we turn one PM pain point into an AI assistant we can actually test. We are not building a production system. We leave with a Pilot Pack: the problem, a baseline, the agent’s instructions, what the PM still decides, one guardrail, one metric, an owner and a first test.”
    Do
    • Name the Scribe, Timekeeper, Builders and Challengers.

  2. 05:00–15:00Map the hours

    “Twenty seconds each: how many hours does this job take you in a normal week?”
    Ask
    • What are the steps of this job, start to finish?
    • How many minutes does each step take?
    • Which steps are repetitive, and which need the PM’s judgment?
    • Which repetitive chunk is the biggest?
    • What would the PM do with that time instead?
    Decide

    The chunk the agent takes, and the baseline: hours per PM per week today.

    Rule

    No number? Take a show-of-hands range and mark it “to be measured in the first test”.

    • How long should the PM’s review of it take?

    • Which facts must come from a system rather than from the AI’s words?

    • What must never appear in the output?
    Do

    Fill the blueprint scaffold from Build the agent. Write the three tests.

    27:00 · Instructions drafted, PM decision named, three tests written
  4. 27:00–42:00Build & challenge
    Split

    The room works as two sub-teams in parallel.

    Builders

    Paste the instructions and the sample data into the approved tool. Run T1, T2 and T3. Record pass or fail and keep the output. No tool? Run “Be the agent”.

    Challengers

    Pressure-test with seven checks: value (who saves what?), data (available and allowed?), quality (what if it is wrong?), tool (approved, with access?), accountability (who signs off?), adoption (would you use it on Monday?), metric (can we really measure it?).

    Say
    “Challengers, your job is to find the reason this pilot would fail, then write the guardrail that prevents it.”
    42:00 · Test results recorded, review time measured, top three risks listed
  5. 42:00–50:00Fix & decide
    Say
    “Builders, what broke? Challengers, what worries you most?”
    Do
    • Add one rule to the instructions for each failed test.
    • Pick the one guardrail and the one metric that matter most.
    • Decide which level the room reached.
    50:00 · Final instructions, one guardrail, hours back per week, level decided
  6. 50:00–60:00Package & commit
    Say
    “Who runs the first test in the next two weeks? If we can’t name a person, which role, and who confirms the name by when?”
    Do
    • Complete owner, first test, start date and report-out.
    • Rehearse the 90-second report-out once.
    • Submit the Pilot Pack and keep a local copy.
    Rule

    If behind, cut solution detail. Never cut owner, first test, metric or guardrail.

Inclusion prompts

  • “Let’s hear from someone who hasn’t spoken yet.”
  • “Who here would use this on Monday? What would stop you?”
  • “What would a PM new to AI need to trust this output?”
  • To an expert: “Please narrate what you type so we can all follow.”
  • “Does anyone think this is the wrong slice? Say it now.”
Facilitator only

Rescue questions

Ask questions; do not hand over answers.

  • Stuck on the problem: “Walk me through last Friday. What did you open first?”
  • Solution too big: “What is the smallest version a PM could run tomorrow with copy and paste?”
  • Output too generic: “Could someone outside this room run the first test from what we wrote?”
  • Time test disappoints: “If checking the output takes as long as doing the job, what should the agent do differently?”

The story in the room

Participants arrive having read Fourteen Days, the KESTREL pre-work story, and one case file. Use the story at three moments.

  • 00:00 · Cold open, 45 seconds. Read aloud: “It’s Thursday, 07:30. Tape-out in 11 days. A brief for steering at 14:00. A status that may not be green. A customer waiting. Minutes nobody sent. A risk log five weeks old. Ten lessons found too late. Our room takes one of them. In 60 minutes we build the agent that gives it back.” Then name the room’s chapter.
  • 27:00 · The time test. Time how long the PM needs to check the agent’s output and compare it with the baseline from the task map. Anything the room noticed in the case file becomes the T3 trust check: does the agent flag it for the PM?
  • Report-out · Close the loop. Each room opens with one line: “On KESTREL, our Thursday would have started differently, because …”. The host calls rooms in the order of the Thursday list: C4, C1, C6, C2, C3, C5.
Build the agent

Blueprint, three tests, and a paper fallback

The scaffold gives every room the same shape and the same safety rules. The room fills in the brackets; the rules are defaults a room may tighten but should not drop.

Agent blueprint scaffold

AGENT BLUEPRINT
Name: [short, memorable]
Job: Helps [which user] to [task] so that [outcome].
Used when: [trigger, e.g. every Friday before the status report, after each project meeting]
Inputs: [what the PM pastes, uploads or connects]
Source of truth: [which system wins when inputs disagree]

===== INSTRUCTIONS (paste into the approved AI tool) =====
ROLE
You are an assistant to a project manager on a cross-functional product development project.

TASK
From the inputs I give you, produce [output] for [audience].
Steps:
1. [ ]
2. [ ]
3. [ ]

RULES
- Use only the information in the inputs. Do not invent facts, dates, names or numbers.
- For every item you report, name the input it came from.
- If inputs are missing, unclear or contradict each other, list them under NEEDS PM CHECK. Do not resolve them yourself.
- Do not make [the decision the PM keeps]. Prepare it and label it PM DECISION.
- Leave out [what must never appear, e.g. personal details, contract terms].
- Keep the output to [length], in a [tone] tone.

OUTPUT FORMAT
[headings or table the PM wants to receive]
NEEDS PM CHECK: [list]
===== END INSTRUCTIONS =====

Human checkpoint: before using the output, the PM checks [ ].
Guardrails: allowed data [ ]. Not allowed [ ]. Tool: approved tool only.
Tests:
T1 Does the job:  [full inputs] -> pass if the PM can use the output after a short review
T2 Time test:     [PM review time] vs [manual baseline] -> pass if clearly less
T3 Trust check:   [a conflict, gap or decision in the data] -> pass if flagged for the PM, not resolved
TestWhat you feed itPass when
T1 Does the jobThe full case fileThe PM can use the output after a short review, and every item names its source.
T2 Time testA stopwatch: the PM’s review of the agent’s outputReview time is clearly below the manual baseline from the task map. The difference is the time returned.
T3 Trust checkA conflict, gap, personal detail or decision in the case fileFlagged for the PM, never smoothed over or decided. A PM only gets time back from output they can trust.

Record pass or fail and what you changed. A failed test followed by a fix is a better report-out than three easy passes.

Be the agent: the paper test

8–10 minutes. Counts as Level 2. Needs no tool, no login and no network.

  1. Pick a “human agent” who did not write the instructions.
  2. Give them only the instructions and the printed sample data.
  3. They produce the output aloud or on paper, following the instructions literally. Where the instructions are silent, they say “my instructions don’t tell me” and stop that step.
  4. Everyone else notes each gap. Each gap becomes one new rule.
  5. Run the trust check: does the agent flag what it cannot resolve? If not, which rule would make it?

Beginners learn quickly from this why precise instructions matter, and experts cannot rush past it.

Tool readiness Open decision

  • Which AI tool is approved for the breakout, and whether participants can build agents in it, is not yet confirmed. It decides whether Level 3 is possible.
  • Rooms use only the fictional KESTREL data, or project data already approved for that tool.
  • No customer or confidential data goes into an unapproved or public tool, in the room or afterwards.
  • Builders test their login during pre-work, not in the room.

Report-out and after the summit Proposal

Report-out script

Room tackled challenge for users. Today it costs baseline. Our agent does what, and the PM still decides what. We reached level; our most useful test result was finding. Every PM gets hours back a week, for decisions, stakeholders or strategy. Owner runs the first test by date, guarded by guardrail.

Prioritization rubric

Regions run a quick vote (“which pilot would you use next month?”, two votes each). A WS2 panel then scores every pack 1–5 on four criteria. Voting model and decision authority are Open decisions.

Ready nowNeeds new data access or an unapproved toolRuns with approved tools and data today
SafeHuman checkpoint or guardrail unclearExplicit checkpoint, tested guardrail, no hidden confidentiality issue
ReusableFits one team’s way of workingWorks across teams and regions with little change

    WS2 compares the regional packs for each challenge and merges the best rules and tests into one v1 agent.

    One owner per challenge; three to five PMs run v1 against the room baseline.

Preparation and open decisions

What has to happen before 5 November

Participants

About 20 minutes, sent one week before

  • Read the story Fourteen Days (12 minutes). It follows KESTREL’s new PM through the week that produced all six challenges.
  • Read your room’s case file at the back of the story and note which parts of the work you would hand to an agent.
  • Map your own week: time each step of this job and mark which steps are repetitive.
  • Bring one idea for the hours back: a decision, a stakeholder or thinking time. No confidential documents.
  • Builders only: log in to the approved tool and paste the scaffold once.

Facilitators

About 60 minutes

  • Attend a 30-minute WS2 briefing.
  • Walk through your card, including its traps. Try the timer. Fill the Pilot Pack with the worked example.
  • Prepare offline copies: card, sample data, blank Pilot Pack.
  • Know your room’s Builder and backup.

Regional leads

  • Assign one card per room and match it to the room’s experience.
  • Name a facilitator, a Builder and a backup for every room.
  • Optionally propose one swap card for WS2 review.
  • Confirm the capture link and the fallback with the site owner.

Card allocation rule Proposal

  • Every room gets one card, assigned before the summit and named in the pre-work.
  • Match cards to experience: entry rooms take C2 or C1; mixed rooms C1, C3, C4 or C5; experienced rooms C6 or C4.
  • Regions with six or more rooms run every card at least once, so each challenge has a version from every region.
  • Regions with fewer rooms pick in this order: C2, C1, C3, C4, C6, C5.
  • Room size: start from 5–8 people Assumption and test 5, 8 and 12 in the mock run. Above 10, split into two rooms on the same card.

Using this kit in the core-team mock run

  • Run C1 (richest traps) in one room and a draft swap card in the other, so both the standard and the swap process are tested.
  • Inject at least two failures from the recovery table, including a tool failure that forces the paper test.
  • Add two checks to the observer scorecard: “time test run and hours back recorded” and “instructions understandable when read aloud to a non-expert”.
  • Pass criteria as in the knowledge base: all mandatory fields, owner, first test, metric and guardrail explicit, finished inside 60 minutes, no hidden confidentiality or tool issue.
